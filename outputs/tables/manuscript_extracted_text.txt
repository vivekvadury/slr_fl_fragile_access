Sea-level rise (SLR) is reshaping coastal communities in ways that conventional inundation accounting fails to capture. Storm surge, coastal erosion,
and recurrent tidal ooding are the most visible effects of rising seas, but their
consequences can also propagate through transportation networks. Roads may
remain mostly dry while alternative routes disappear, people are forced onto
fewer trac corridors, and access to schools, grocery stores, and other essential
services becomes less reliable. This study examines such hidden degradation in
South Florida and asks who bears its burden.
Recent work shows that SLR can disconnect people from essential services
before their homes are directly inundated. Logan et al. (
1
) define risk of
isolation as the loss of road-network connectivity between a residential block
and its nearest essential service, showing that the population at risk of isolation
can exceed the population at risk of inundation. Best et al. (
2
) further show
that this risk is socially uneven, with demographic patterns that vary across
SLR levels. Together, this research establishes that transportation disruption
is an important and unequal dimension of climate exposure.
However, a binary isolated/connected classification collapses important
differences among connected places. A block with several independent routes
to essential services is not in the same position as a block that depends on a
single remaining route. Both are connected, but the second is already fragile:
one additional ooded road segment could turn degraded access into isolation.
This intermediate state matters for adaptation planning because it identifies
places where intervention could preserve access before complete disconnection
occurs.
Empirically, the length of inaccessible road can far exceed the length of
physically ooded road, and networks with greater redundancy are better able
to absorb the same disturbance without equivalent losses in access (
3
). Network
studies also show that ood impacts can be highly nonlinear: access may change
little after some road disruptions, then decline sharply once ooding reaches
connectors that many routes depend on (
4
,
5
). Measuring access degradation
as a sequence, rather than only as final isolation or inundation, can therefore
identify where redundancy has already been lost and where continued disruption
is most likely to become consequential.
Service degradation from SLR, however, does not unfold on a blank social
landscape. If the communities losing reliable access are also those least equipped
to prepare for, absorb, or recover from disruption, then network fragility and
social vulnerability compound in ways that neither lens captures alone (
6
).
Therefore, adaptation planning must measure both the stages through which
access degrades and the social geography of the communities experiencing those
changes.
This paper extends the isolation framework (
1
) by treating access loss as a
sequence rather than a binary outcome. I classify census blocks as redundant,
fragile, isolated, or inundated under baseline conditions and under NOAA sealevel-rise scenarios. Blocks with two or more edge-disjoint dry paths to essential
services are classified as redundant; blocks with exactly one dry path are fragile;
blocks with no dry path are isolated; and blocks whose centroids intersect the
inundation layer are inundated. I then aggregate block-level transitions to
block groups and estimate whether socially vulnerable communities face higher
probabilities of moving from one access state to a worse one.
Significance
Sea-level rise affects communities
not only by ooding land, but also by
weakening the road networks that
connect residents to essential
services. This study shows how
access can degrade in stages: places
may first lose redundant routes, then
become dependent on a single fragile
path, and only later become isolated
or inundated. Measuring these
transitions reveals risks that
inundation-only approaches miss.
Linking these network changes to
social vulnerability shows where
sea-level rise may compound existing
inequalities and whereand
howadaptation policy could act
before everyday connectivity fails.

South Florida provides a useful setting for this analysis.
Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach counties combine
acute SLR exposure, heterogeneous road-network structure, and large social differences in income, race, ethnicity,
age, housing tenure, and vehicle access. These conditions
make it possible to ask not only where SLR removes
transportation access, but also whether different measures
of access loss make different inequalities visible. By linking
access transitions to social vulnerability, this paper shows
where|and for whom|SLR erodes the redundancy that
keeps everyday access possible before degradation becomes
disconnection. This analysis make it possible to ask
where SLR-induced access losses concentrate, whether
socially vulnerable communities face higher probabilities
of degradation, and whether different definitions of access
loss reveal different inequalities.
Results
Sea-level rise degrades access before isolation or inundation.
Analyzing lost road redundancy highlights new geographic communities at risk of SLR.
Discussion
Materials and Methods
Study area and units of analysis.
 The study area includes Miami-Dade,
Broward, and Palm Beach counties in South Florida. These counties are
analyzed together because SLR exposure, road-network structure, and
adaptation planning are regional rather than strictly municipal processes.
The census block is the primary unit for measuring access states, because
block centroids provide a fine-grained origin point for assessing whether
residents retain dry road access to essential services. The block group
is the primary unit for the vulnerability analysis, because social and
demographic covariates are measured consistently at that geography. I
therefore first classify access states at the block level, then aggregate
block-level transitions to block groups for statistical analysis.

Essential services include primary schools and fire stations, following
prior work on SLR-induced isolation. Public and private primary schools
are combined with filtered fire-station locations into a single essentialservices layer. These facilities capture routine, place-based access
demands: primary schools represent everyday outbound travel needs, while
fire stations represent critical inbound emergency response. Both block
centroids and service points are snapped to the retained road network.
I retain primary schools and fire stations within 10 km of the retained
tri-county road-network boundary, including nearby facilities outside the
study area. This allows boundary blocks to reach plausible out-of-county
destinations, reducing the risk of understating their connectivity.

Social vulnerability indicators.
 I use 2022 ACS 5-year block-group
estimates to measure social vulnerability. The selected indicators are
percent non-Hispanic Black, percent Hispanic, renter share, log median
household income, share of residents age 65 and older, and share
of households without access to a vehicle. These indicators capture
social, economic, housing, age-related, and mobility dimensions of social
vulnerability that may shape the capacity to prepare for, absorb, or recover
from transportation disruption.
The indicators are not intended to represent vulnerability as a single
index. Instead, I estimate separate associations for each covariate
because different dimensions of social vulnerability may relate to different
stages of access degradation. All indicators are standardized before
estimation, meaning coefficients and marginal effects can be compared
across covariates. Because vulnerability measures can embed strong
assumptions about which social identities matter, Table

indicator selection transparent by connecting each covariate to a specific
dimension of SLR-induced access risk.

Interpretation and limitations.
 The models estimate associations between block-group social characteristics and access-transition probabilities.
Therefore, they should not be interpreted as causal estimates. The
purpose is to identify whether different dimensions of social vulnerability
are associated with different stages of SLR-induced access degradation,
conditional on county and SLR-scenario fixed effects.
Several simplifying assumptions should guide interpretation. The
network analysis is centroid-based and does not capture within-block
variation in residence location or exposure. This binary coding is a
deliberate simplification. In reality, a road's usability under ooding depends
on its design and elevation, the depth and duration of inundation, and
the speed of post-event maintenance, none of which the intersection rule
captures. The essential-service set is limited to primary schools and fire
stations, so the estimates should be interpreted as access degradation to
selected essential serviceswith solid rationale for inclusionrather than
to all destinations required for maintaining normal connectivity and access.
